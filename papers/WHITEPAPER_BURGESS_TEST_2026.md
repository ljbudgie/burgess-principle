## Abstract

Between August 2024 and July 2026, three regulatory instruments established a requirement for meaningful human involvement in automated decision-making affecting individuals: the UK Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (Arts 22A–22D UK GDPR), the EU AI Act 2024/1689 (Arts 14, 26, 86), and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. A fourth instrument—the proposed US AI Civil Rights Act of 2025—signals the same direction. None provides an operational definition of “meaningful human involvement.” In June 2026 the EU deferred its high-risk obligations expressly because the standards and tooling required to verify compliance were not yet available.

This paper presents the Burgess Principle—a binary test applicable across jurisdictions and sectors—and demonstrates its operationality through an empirical record. As at 18 July 2026 the framework had produced 100 classified findings across energy, financial services, enforcement, policing, healthcare, government and platform sectors (78 NULL, 8 SOVEREIGN, 10 AMBIGUOUS, 4 confirmed resolutions). Twelve institutions are scored on a per-dimension accountability register (mean 2.3/20). One classification has been tested before an independent statutory tribunal, which reached a concordant outcome.

The test asks one question of any institution exercising power over an identified person:
“Was a human member of the team able to personally review the specific facts of my specific situation?”

The answer is classified SOVEREIGN, NULL or AMBIGUOUS. This revision documents the taxonomy evolution (v1.0→v1.3), three verbatim institutional admissions of automated processing, a financial-regulator FOI confirming the regulatory gap, and the first independent adjudication converging on a framework finding.
